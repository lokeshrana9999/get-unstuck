# Evidence review: "Getting Started with Executive Dysfunction"

This checks each strategy and claim in `breakdown_v2.md` against peer-reviewed research. It goes in the chart's own order, preceded by the chart's underlying framing (ADHD, executive dysfunction, dopamine).

## How to read this

**Evidence levels** rate the claim *as the chart uses it*: a quick self-help step to help someone start a task.

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Strong** | Several good-quality meta-analyses or RCTs support it, with consistent results |
| **Moderate** | RCTs or meta-analyses support it, but with real limits: small effects, a different population, lab-only results, or lots of variation between studies |
| **Weak** | Indirect, preliminary, correlational, or only from a neighbouring area of research |
| **None** | I found no study of the claim (this is different from evidence that it doesn't work) |
| **Contradicted** | Good evidence says the claim or the mechanism it relies on is false |

Many items get two ratings: one for the specific claim, and one for the nearby principle that does have research behind it. That gap is the main finding of this review.

**Caveats that apply throughout:**

1. **Almost nothing here was tested on adults with ADHD trying to start tasks.** Most evidence comes from students, healthy adults, children with ADHD in lab tasks, or other conditions such as BPD, depression and substance use.
2. **ADHD-specific psychological evidence is mostly for *packages*.** Multi-week CBT or DBT-based group programmes, delivered by therapists, combine many of the chart's skills. No trial isolates any single tip.
3. **How the sources were found and checked.** Research was done by web search. Each DOI below was then checked against the Crossref registry and resolves to the stated paper. Items with only a URL were seen directly in search results or fetched pages. Citations that couldn't be verified are listed at the end and **not** used for any rating.
4. **Effect sizes** (d, g, r) are as reported in the abstracts or by the study authors. I did not recompute them.

## Part A. The chart's underlying framing

### A1. Framing ADHD as "executive dysfunction"
- **Claim (implicit):** Trouble getting started is executive dysfunction, as seen in ADHD.
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** that ADHD groups show executive function (EF) weaknesses on average. **Contradicted** as the defining or universal cause of ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - A meta-analysis of 83 studies found ADHD groups weaker on EF tasks, most strongly on response inhibition, vigilance, working memory and planning. The effects were moderate, and the authors concluded EF weaknesses are "neither necessary nor sufficient" to cause all cases of ADHD (Willcutt et al. 2005).
  - Only a subset of people with ADHD have any given neurocognitive deficit, and the ADHD and non-ADHD groups overlap a lot (Nigg et al. 2005).
  - Barkley's influential model puts weak behavioural inhibition at the core, with EF problems following from it. It was offered as a theory to be tested (Barkley 1997).
  - Lab EF tests and EF questionnaires barely correlate (median r ≈ .19; Toplak et al. 2013), so they probably measure different things. Everyday "executive dysfunction", which is what the chart addresses, is closer to what the questionnaires measure.
- **Citations:**
  - Willcutt, Doyle, Nigg, Faraone & Pennington (2005). Validity of the executive function theory of ADHD: a meta-analytic review. *Biological Psychiatry* 57(11). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2005.02.006
  - Nigg, Willcutt, Doyle & Sonuga-Barke (2005). Causal heterogeneity in ADHD: do we need neuropsychologically impaired subtypes? *Biological Psychiatry* 57(11). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biopsych.2004.08.025
  - Barkley (1997). Behavioral inhibition, sustained attention, and executive functions. *Psychological Bulletin* 121(1). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.121.1.65
  - Toplak, West & Stanovich (2013). Practitioner review: Do performance-based measures and ratings of executive function assess the same construct? *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 54(2). https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12001
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. "Executive dysfunction" is a useful plain-language description. But ADHD is a heterogeneous condition, not a single EF deficit, and EF problems also occur without ADHD (in depression, autism, sleep loss and other conditions).

### A2. "Paralysis" and "inertia" as categories
- **Claim (implicit):** "PARALYSIS: I don't know how to do it" and "INERTIA: I can't stop doing something else" are distinct, recognisable states.
- **Evidence:**
  - **None** as clinical constructs. "ADHD paralysis" and "inertia" are lay terms.
  - **Moderate** that adults with ADHD *report* hyperfocus and difficulty disengaging. **Weak and mixed** in lab task-switching studies.
- **What research shows:**
  - Procrastination, the nearest researched construct, is linked in students mainly to *inattention* symptoms. It is not an official ADHD symptom (Niermann & Scheres 2014).
  - In adults with ADHD, problems remembering to act on intentions partly explain the link between ADHD and procrastination (Altgassen et al. 2019). Forgetting may matter as much as "paralysis".
  - In questionnaires, adults with ADHD report more hyperfocus than others (Hupfeld et al. 2019). A review notes hyperfocus has no agreed definition and little lab evidence behind it (Ashinoff & Abu-Akel 2019).
  - Health sites such as Cleveland Clinic describe "ADHD paralysis" as a colloquial, non-diagnostic term.
- **Citations:**
  - Niermann & Scheres (2014). The relation between procrastination and symptoms of ADHD in undergraduate students. *Int J Methods Psychiatr Res* 23(4). https://doi.org/10.1002/mpr.1440
  - Altgassen, Scheres & Edel (2019). Prospective memory (partially) mediates the link between ADHD symptoms and procrastination. *ADHD Atten Def Hyp Disord* 11. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-018-0273-x
  - Hupfeld, Abagis & Shah (2019). Living "in the zone": hyperfocus in adult ADHD. *ADHD Atten Def Hyp Disord* 11(2). https://doi.org/10.1007/s12402-018-0272-y
  - Ashinoff & Abu-Akel (2019/2021). Hyperfocus: the forgotten frontier of attention. *Psychological Research* 85(1). https://doi.org/10.1007/s00426-019-01245-8
  - Cleveland Clinic, "ADHD paralysis". https://health.clevelandclinic.org/adhd-paralysis
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. These terms are relatable but sound more clinical than they are.

### A3. The "dopamine" framing ("DIY DOPAMINE", "Follow the dopamine")
- **Claim (implicit):** ADHD involves a dopamine shortfall, which you can top up with pleasant activities, and that helps you get started.
- **Evidence:**
  - **Moderate** that dopamine and reward systems are involved in ADHD.
  - **Contradicted** for "dopamine = pleasure hit".
  - **None** for topping up dopamine with activities to help start a task.
  - **None** for the "dopamine menu" idea.
- **What research shows:**
  - **Dopamine in ADHD.** In 53 unmedicated adults with ADHD, a PET study found lower dopamine transporter and D2/D3 receptor availability in reward regions, and these correlated with inattention (Volkow et al. 2009). The sample was small and the design cross-sectional.
  - **Reward anticipation.** The dopamine transfer deficit theory proposes weaker *anticipatory* dopamine signals to cues that predict reward (Tripp & Wickens 2008). It is a hypothesis, and it predicts trouble with *delayed* rewards, which is not the same as "needing more hits".
  - **A broad picture.** The international consensus statement describes ADHD as heterogeneous and polygenic, not a single-neurotransmitter deficiency (Faraone et al. 2021).
  - **Dopamine is about wanting, not liking.** Dopamine supports "wanting" (motivational salience), not hedonic "liking". Animals with almost all brain dopamine removed still "like" sugar normally (Berridge & Robinson 1998, 2016).
  - **Prediction, not pleasure.** Dopamine neurons signal *reward prediction errors*, i.e. outcomes better or worse than expected (Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997). The "pleasure chemical" and "hit" metaphors get its function wrong.
  - **The "dopamine menu."** It started as a 2020 How to ADHD YouTube video. I found no peer-reviewed study of it. A neuropharmacologist and a psychologist writing in *The Conversation* answered "maybe" to whether it works, based on general neuroscience rather than trials (Davidson & Hutchinson 2023; not peer-reviewed).
- **Citations:**
  - Volkow et al. (2009). Evaluating dopamine reward pathway in ADHD. *JAMA* 302(10). https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2009.1308
  - Tripp & Wickens (2008). Dopamine transfer deficit. *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 49(7). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2007.01851.x
  - Faraone et al. (2021). The World Federation of ADHD International Consensus Statement. *Neurosci Biobehav Rev* 128. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2021.01.022
  - Berridge & Robinson (1998). What is the role of dopamine in reward? *Brain Res Rev* 28. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0165-0173(98)00019-8
  - Berridge & Robinson (2016). Liking, wanting, and the incentive-sensitization theory of addiction. *American Psychologist*. https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000059
  - Schultz, Dayan & Montague (1997). A neural substrate of prediction and reward. *Science* 275. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.275.5306.1593
  - Davidson & Hutchinson (2023). Dopamine menus: the science behind the trend. *The Conversation*. https://theconversation.com/dopamine-menus-the-science-behind-the-trend-and-how-it-might-help-people-with-adhd-218970
- **Pop-science flag: STRONG.** "DIY dopamine" turns a partial, mostly correlational finding into a "refuel the tank" metaphor that misdescribes what dopamine does. Many of the activities in that box may still help (see Part E). If they do, it's through ordinary mood, arousal, movement and reward effects, not a dopamine top-up you can steer.

### A4. Where self-help fits with evidence-based ADHD treatment
- **Claim (implicit):** The chart's self-help steps are a way to manage executive dysfunction.
- **Evidence:** **Strong** that medication is the most effective short-term treatment for adult ADHD. **Low to moderate** for CBT-type psychological treatment in adults.
- **What research shows:**
  - **Medication.** A network meta-analysis of 133 RCTs found stimulants and atomoxetine beat placebo for adults. Amphetamines were the preferred first-line option for adults, and modafinil did not beat placebo (Cortese et al. 2018). The trials were mostly short (about 12 weeks).
  - **CBT.** A Cochrane review found *low-quality* evidence of short-term benefit from CBT for adults (Lopez et al. 2018).
  - **Packaged ADHD-specific CBT.** Two RCTs of multi-component ADHD-specific CBT beat active controls: Safren et al. 2010 (86 medicated adults) and Solanto et al. 2010 (88 adults). Both taught several of the chart's skills together, including breaking tasks down, planning and reminders.
  - **The "Wellbeing" box.** The chart's "PHYSICAL: meds" item is the single best-evidenced entry in it.
- **Citations:**
  - Cortese et al. (2018). Comparative efficacy and tolerability of medications for ADHD. *Lancet Psychiatry* 5(9). https://doi.org/10.1016/S2215-0366(18)30269-4
  - Lopez et al. (2018). Cognitive-behavioural interventions for ADHD in adults. *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*. https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD010840.pub2
  - Safren et al. (2010). CBT vs relaxation with educational support for medication-treated adults with ADHD. *JAMA* 304(8). https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2010.1192
  - Solanto et al. (2010). Efficacy of meta-cognitive therapy for adult ADHD. *Am J Psychiatry* 167(8). https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2009.09081123
  - NICE guideline NG87 (ADHD diagnosis and management). https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng87 (I couldn't load the page, so the recommendation wording isn't verified here.)
- **Pop-science flag:** Mild. The chart doesn't claim to replace treatment. Its tips are low-risk complements, best treated as untested parts of the CBT approach.

## Part B. Steps ①–③: knowing what to do, starting, and why not

### B1. PRIORITIZE: "Use an important/urgent matrix" (the Eisenhower matrix)
- **Evidence:** **None** for the matrix itself. **Moderate** that the bias it targets is real. **Moderate** that time management in general is linked to well-being.
- **What research shows:**
  - I found no controlled trial of the Eisenhower matrix, only small uncontrolled reports.
  - The "mere urgency effect" is real: across five experiments, people picked urgent tasks over more important, better-paid ones, even when the urgency was illusory (Zhu, Yang & Hsee 2018).
  - An independent replication was published in 2026 (Mills et al.). A secondary summary says it found the effect weaker but present. I did not read the paper's own results.
  - A meta-analysis of 158 studies (N > 53,000) found time management moderately linked to job performance, and more strongly linked to well-being. The data are mostly correlational, from non-ADHD samples (Aeon et al. 2021).
  - So the matrix targets a documented bias, but nobody has shown that it fixes that bias.
- **Citations:**
  - Zhu, Yang & Hsee (2018). The mere urgency effect. *J Consumer Research* 45(3). https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy008
  - Mills et al. (2026). An independent replication study of the mere urgency effect. *J Consumer Research*. https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucag026
  - Aeon, Faber & Panaccio (2021). Does time management work? A meta-analysis. *PLOS ONE* 16(1). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0245066
- **Pop-science flag:** Blogs present the urgency studies as validating the matrix. They don't: they show the problem, not the fix. "Urgent vs important" comes from an Eisenhower speech, popularised by Covey.

### B2. "Pick the top 3 things or 1 next thing"
- **Evidence:** **None** for the numbers. **Weak** for the logic of reducing choice.
- **What research shows:**
  - No study tests capping a to-do list at three items.
  - The nearest research, on choice overload, is mixed. One meta-analysis found a mean effect near zero (Scheibehenne et al. 2010). A later one found overload under specific conditions, such as hard tasks, unclear preferences, or a goal of saving effort (Chernev et al. 2015). Those conditions fit an overwhelmed person reasonably well.
  - "1 next thing" overlaps with implementation intentions (see B3), which *do* have evidence.
- **Citations:**
  - Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010). Can there ever be too many options? *J Consumer Research* 37(3). https://doi.org/10.1086/651235
  - Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman (2015). Choice overload: a conceptual review and meta-analysis. *J Consumer Psychology* 25(2). https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/research/detail/2015/when-product-assortment-leads-to-choice-overload-a-conceptual

### B3. BREAK IT DOWN: "What's the smallest, easiest, measurable first step?"
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** for the principle (specific goals, near-term subgoals, if-then plans), from non-ADHD research. **Moderate** for ADHD CBT packages that include it.
- **What research shows:**
  - Specific goals beat vague "do your best" goals across decades of mostly workplace and lab research (Locke & Latham 2002).
  - Near-term subgoals built competence, self-efficacy and interest better than distant goals, in children with maths difficulties (Bandura & Schunk 1981). That was one experiment.
  - Implementation intentions, i.e. "when X, I will do Y" plans, had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals, including on getting started (d = 0.65 across 94 tests; Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006).
  - Making a concrete plan reduces the mental intrusion of unfinished goals (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011).
  - Adult ADHD CBT trials teach task breakdown as one part of the package (Safren 2010; Solanto 2010, see A4). Neither trial isolates it.
  - "Measurable" and "smallest" fit this research. "Easiest first" has no specific support.
- **Citations:**
  - Locke & Latham (2002). Building a practically useful theory of goal setting and task motivation. *American Psychologist* 57(9). https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705
  - Bandura & Schunk (1981). Cultivating competence, self-efficacy, and intrinsic interest through proximal self-motivation. *JPSP* 41(3). https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.3.586
  - Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis. *Adv Exp Soc Psychol* 38. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1
  - Masicampo & Baumeister (2011). Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals. *JPSP* 101(4). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192

### B4. "Use Goblin Tools!"
- **Evidence:** **None.**
- **What research shows:** I found no peer-reviewed study of Goblin Tools, or of any AI task-breakdown app for ADHD. Coverage is limited to app listings and advocacy articles, such as CHADD's *Attention* magazine, December 2024. If it helps, it would be by producing the specific, near-term subgoals described in B3. That is plausible but untested.
- **Citations:** CHADD *Attention* (Dec 2024), descriptive only. https://chadd.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/ATTN_12_2024-Harnessing-Artificial-Intelligence-to-Live-Better-with-ADHD.pdf

### B5. "Can you do it for just 10 minutes?" (and "just start" logic)
- **Evidence:**
  - **None** for the specific time box.
  - **Weak to moderate** for structured procrastination treatments in general.
  - The usual justification, the Zeigarnik effect, is **contradicted**.
- **What research shows:**
  - I found no controlled trial of "commit to just N minutes".
  - Procrastination interventions do reduce procrastination:
    - van Eerde & Klingsieck 2018: 24 studies.
    - Rozental et al. 2018: 12 RCTs, g = 0.34 overall, with high risk of bias.
    - Both cover students and general adults, not ADHD.
  - The popular rationale, "your brain can't stand unfinished tasks" (the Zeigarnik memory effect), failed in a 2025 meta-analysis. The related *Ovsiankina* effect did hold: people tend to resume interrupted tasks (Ghibellini & Meier 2025). That is a better reason for "just start".
  - Behavioural activation, which schedules small graded activities, works well for depression (Ekers et al. 2014). It is a plausible analogue, but not ADHD evidence.
- **Citations:**
  - van Eerde & Klingsieck (2018). Overcoming procrastination? A meta-analysis of intervention studies. *Educational Research Review* 25. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.002
  - Rozental et al. (2018). Targeting procrastination using psychological treatments. *Frontiers in Psychology* 9. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01588
  - Ghibellini & Meier (2025). Interruption, recall and resumption: a meta-analysis of the Zeigarnik and Ovsiankina effects. *Humanities & Social Sciences Communications* 12. https://doi.org/10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w
  - Ekers et al. (2014). Behavioural activation for depression; an update of meta-analysis. *PLOS ONE* 9. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate, for the "Zeigarnik effect" explanation often attached to this tip.

### B6. Branch reasons: "It feels too hard or I'm afraid of failing" (PERFECTIONISM) and "I don't want to do it" (MOTIVATION)
- **Evidence:**
  - **Strong** that task aversiveness and low self-efficacy go with procrastination.
  - **Weak** for fear of failure as a direct driver.
  - **Contradicted** for perfectionism in general as a cause.
- **What research shows:**
  - Steel's meta-analysis (691 correlations, mostly students and general adults) found procrastination linked to:
    - finding the task aversive (r ≈ .40)
    - low self-efficacy (r ≈ −.38)
    - fear of failure only weakly (r ≈ .18), which appeared to work through low confidence
    - perfectionism, overall, not at all (r ≈ −.03)
  - A later meta-analysis refined this:
    - *Perfectionistic concerns* (fear of mistakes, self-criticism) correlate with more procrastination (r = .23).
    - *Perfectionistic strivings* (high standards) correlate with less (r = −.22).
  - So the chart is right to separate "too hard / unpleasant" (strong predictors) from vague "perfectionism". Sending both to BREAK IT DOWN suits the self-efficacy part.
- **Citations:**
  - Steel (2007). The nature of procrastination. *Psychological Bulletin* 133(1). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65
  - Sirois, Molnar & Hirsch (2017). A meta-analytic and conceptual update on procrastination and multidimensional perfectionism. *European J Personality* 31(2). https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2098
- **Pop-science flag:** "Perfectionism causes procrastination" is overstated. Feeling unable to do the task, and finding it unpleasant, are the robust drivers.

### B7. MINDFULNESS: "Take 5 minutes to sit quietly and consider why"
- **Evidence:** **Weak** for a 5-minute "consider why" pause. **Moderate** (low-to-moderate quality) for formal multi-week mindfulness programmes in adult ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - Structured mindfulness programmes, usually about 8 weeks, reduce self-rated ADHD symptoms. Examples:
    - Cairncross & Miller 2016: inattention d ≈ −0.66.
    - Poissant et al. 2020: 14 studies, adults.
    - Kim & Jung 2025: adults, self-rated SMD ≈ 0.48.
  - The best caution is Oliva et al. 2021. Effects were medium-to-large against waitlists but **low or negligible against active controls**. After correcting for publication bias, there was **no significant effect on executive function**.
  - Brief mindfulness in non-ADHD samples has a small effect on negative mood. It falls to about zero after correcting for publication bias (Schumer et al. 2018).
  - The chart's "consider why" is really *reflective introspection*: closer to self-monitoring or functional analysis than to mindfulness (non-judgemental attention to the present). It's a reasonable step, but mindfulness trial results don't apply to it.
- **Citations:**
  - Cairncross & Miller (2016). The effectiveness of mindfulness-based therapies for ADHD: a meta-analytic review. *J Atten Disord*. https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054715625301
  - Poissant, Moreno, Potvin & Mendrek (2020). A meta-analysis of mindfulness-based interventions in adults with ADHD. *Mindfulness*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-020-01458-8
  - Oliva et al. (2021). The efficacy of mindfulness-based interventions in ADHD beyond core symptoms. *J Affective Disorders* 292. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2021.05.068
  - Kim & Jung (2025). Mindfulness-based interventions for adults with ADHD: systematic review and meta-analysis. *Medicine* 104(37). https://doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000044308
  - Schumer, Lindsay & Creswell (2018). Brief mindfulness training for negative affectivity. *J Consult Clin Psychol* 86(7). https://doi.org/10.1037/ccp0000324
- **Pop-science flag:** Mild. The chart labels introspection as "mindfulness".

## Part C. MAKE IT MORE TOLERABLE

### C1. "Listen to music or an audiobook"
- **Evidence:**
  - **Contradicted or weak** for music or audiobooks during thinking-heavy work.
  - **Moderate** for white or pink *noise* in ADHD, with a small effect.
  - **Moderate** (one field study) for audiobooks used as a *bundled treat* (see C2).
- **What research shows:**
  - **Music in healthy adults.** A meta-analysis found background music has no overall effect. That average hides mixed results: music harmed reading and slightly harmed memory, but helped mood and sport (Kämpfe et al. 2011).
  - **Noise in ADHD.** White noise helped children with ADHD on a memory task and *hurt* controls (Söderlund et al. 2007). A 2024 meta-analysis (13 studies) confirmed a small benefit in ADHD (g ≈ 0.25) and a small harm in non-ADHD groups. The authors rated certainty moderate, and the tasks were brief lab tasks (Nigg et al. 2024).
  - **Audiobooks during a task.** Untested. They probably compete with any task that involves language.
- **Citations:**
  - Kämpfe, Sedlmeier & Renkewitz (2011). The impact of background music on adult listeners: a meta-analysis. *Psychology of Music* 39(4). https://doi.org/10.1177/0305735610376261
  - Söderlund, Sikström & Smart (2007). Listen to the noise: noise is beneficial for cognitive performance in ADHD. *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 48(8). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1469-7610.2007.01749.x
  - Nigg et al. (2024). Do white noise or pink noise help with task performance in youth with ADHD? *JAACAP* 63(8). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2023.12.014
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. "Music helps ADHD focus" blurs noise and music together. Brown noise, also widely recommended, has no studies.

### C2. "Accomodate the task to make it less bad"
- **Evidence:** **Weak to moderate.** It is too vague to test as written. Temptation bundling (pairing a chore with a treat) has field evidence, but for exercise.
- **What research shows:**
  - Gym-goers who could only listen to gripping audiobooks *at the gym* visited 51% more often. The effect faded over time (Milkman et al. 2014; N = 226).
  - A much larger follow-up (N = 6,792) found that *teaching* people to bundle added only a modest benefit (Kirgios et al. 2020).
  - Neither study included ADHD participants or looked at starting tasks.
- **Citations:**
  - Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014). Holding the Hunger Games hostage at the gym. *Management Science* 60(2). https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2013.1784
  - Kirgios et al. (2020). Teaching temptation bundling to boost exercise: a field experiment. *OBHDP* 161. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2020.09.003

### C3. "Pretend to be excited about it, find the fun"
- **Evidence:** **Weak** for this use.
- **What research shows:**
  - Saying "I am excited" before karaoke, public speaking or a maths test improved self-reported excitement and performance compared with trying to calm down (Brooks 2014). But that technique relabels *anxious arousal* before a performance. The chart applies it to boredom and low arousal, where the mechanism hasn't been tested.
  - Gamification meta-analysis: small positive effects on learning motivation (g ≈ .36), less stable in the more rigorous studies (Sailer & Homner 2020). These came from designed systems, not self-made fun.
- **Citations:**
  - Brooks (2014). Get excited: reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement. *J Exp Psychol: General* 143(3). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035325
  - Sailer & Homner (2020). The gamification of learning: a meta-analysis. *Educ Psychol Review* 32. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. A pre-performance anxiety finding is being stretched to cover boring chores.

### C4. "Decide on a reward for after"
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** that rewards help people with ADHD perform. Partly **contradicted** for the "after" timing: delayed rewards lose more of their pull in ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - A review of 22 studies found reinforcement improves performance and motivation in children with and without ADHD, sometimes more in ADHD (Luman et al. 2005).
  - People with ADHD discount delayed rewards more steeply (meta-analysis of case-control studies, d ≈ 0.43; Jackson & MacKillop 2016). Delay aversion is one of the two pathways in Sonuga-Barke's model (2002).
  - In practice: a reward at the end of a long task is a weak motivator. Small, frequent rewards close to the effort fit the evidence better.
  - Expected tangible rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation for tasks that are already interesting (Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999). That matters little for genuinely aversive chores.
  - Most of this evidence is from children in lab settings. Self-administered rewards in adults with ADHD are untested.
- **Citations:**
  - Luman, Oosterlaan & Sergeant (2005). The impact of reinforcement contingencies on AD/HD. *Clinical Psychology Review* 25(2). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2004.11.001
  - Jackson & MacKillop (2016). ADHD and monetary delay discounting: a meta-analysis of case-control studies. *Biol Psychiatry CNNI* 1(4). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bpsc.2016.01.007
  - Sonuga-Barke (2002). Psychological heterogeneity in AD/HD: a dual pathway model. *Behavioural Brain Research* 130. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0166-4328(01)00432-6
  - Deci, Koestner & Ryan (1999). Effects of extrinsic rewards on intrinsic motivation: a meta-analytic review. *Psychological Bulletin* 125(6). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627

### C5. "Change of scenery"
- **Evidence:** **Weak.** Exposure to *nature* has low-to-moderate evidence. I found no direct evidence for simply moving to a different room or a café.
- **What research shows:**
  - A systematic review found time in nature modestly improved working memory and cognitive flexibility (Stevenson et al. 2018).
  - Seventeen children with ADHD concentrated better after a 20-minute park walk than after urban walks (Faber Taylor & Kuo 2009). The sample was tiny.
- **Citations:**
  - Stevenson, Schilhab & Bentsen (2018). Attention Restoration Theory II. *J Toxicol Environ Health B* 21(4). https://doi.org/10.1080/10937404.2018.1505571
  - Faber Taylor & Kuo (2009). Children with attention deficits concentrate better after walk in the park. *J Atten Disord* 12(5). https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054708323000

### C6. "Wear the 'Scientist Hat'- they do this task!"
- **Evidence:** **Weak.** It's unclear exactly what the chart means. The closest evidence is a single unreplicated study in young children.
- **What research shows:**
  - In the "Batman effect" study, 180 children aged 4 and 6 kept working at a boring task longest when pretending to be a capable character (White et al. 2017). I found no replication.
  - In adults, "distanced" self-talk (using your own name or "you") helps regulate stress (Kross et al. 2014). That is about stress, not starting tasks.
- **Citations:**
  - White et al. (2017). The "Batman effect": improving perseverance in young children. *Child Development* 88(5). https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.12695
  - Kross et al. (2014). Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters. *JPSP* 106(2). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173

### C7. "Body double with a friend"
- **Evidence:** **None** from controlled studies. **Weak** (survey) evidence that people find it helpful.
- **What research shows:**
  - A 2024 survey of 220 people, mostly neurodivergent, describes how they use body doubling and what they feel it helps with: getting started, isolation and shame (Eagle & Ringland 2024). That shows it's popular, not that it works.
  - A 2025 preprint states that no controlled trials exist in adults with ADHD.
  - Underlying theory, social facilitation: having others present slightly helps *simple* tasks and slightly hurts *complex* ones (Bond & Titus 1983; 241 studies). That suggests body doubling suits routine chores better than demanding thinking.
- **Citations:**
  - Eagle & Ringland (2024). "It was something I naturally found worked and heard about later": an investigation of body doubling with neurodivergent participants. *ACM TACCESS*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3689648
  - Bond & Titus (1983). Social facilitation: a meta-analysis of 241 studies. *Psychological Bulletin* 94(2). https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.94.2.265
  - Preprint, not peer-reviewed: "You Are Not Alone: Designing Body Doubling for ADHD in Virtual Reality" (2025). https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.12153
- **Pop-science flag: STRONG.** Body doubling is often called "evidence-based" or "proven for ADHD". It isn't yet, though it is low-risk.

## Part D. WELLBEING

### D1. "Are your physical & emotional needs met right now?" (meds, food, rest, sensory, water, movement; social, shame, fear, anxiety)
- **Evidence (varies by item):**

| Item | Evidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Meds | **Strong** | See A4 |
| Rest (sleep) | **Strong** | Non-ADHD samples |
| Movement | **Moderate** | See E3 |
| Water | **Moderate**, small effect | Mainly matters past about 2% fluid loss |
| Emotion → avoidance | **Moderate** | Correlational |
| Sensory | **Emerging** | Mostly child samples |
| Food as willpower fuel | **Contradicted** | See E2 |

- **What research shows:**
  - **Sleep.** Short-term sleep deprivation reliably impairs most cognitive areas, with the biggest effect on attention (Lim & Dinges 2010).
  - **Water.** Dehydration has a small overall effect on cognition. It grows beyond about 2% body-mass loss, which most people at a desk won't reach (Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford 2018).
  - **Food.** Glucose effects on decisions are domain-specific, with no general "fuel" effect (Orquin & Kurzban 2016). Hunger probably matters through discomfort and mood instead.
  - **Emotion.** Procrastination is well described as putting short-term mood repair first (Sirois & Pychyl 2013). That fits the chart's emotional-needs branch.
  - **Sensory.** Large differences in sensory processing between ADHD and control groups, but only 7 of 30 studies were in adults, and studies varied a lot (Jurek et al. 2025).
- **Citations:**
  - Lim & Dinges (2010). A meta-analysis of the impact of short-term sleep deprivation on cognitive variables. *Psychological Bulletin* 136(3). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018883
  - Wittbrodt & Millard-Stafford (2018). Dehydration impairs cognitive performance: a meta-analysis. *Med Sci Sports Exerc*. https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000001682
  - Orquin & Kurzban (2016). A meta-analysis of blood glucose effects on human decision making. *Psychological Bulletin* 142(5). https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000035
  - Sirois & Pychyl (2013). Procrastination and the priority of short-term mood regulation. *Soc Personal Psychol Compass* 7(2). https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011
  - Jurek et al. (2025). Sensory processing in individuals with ADHD compared with control populations. *JAACAP*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jaac.2025.02.019
- **Verdict:** This is one of the chart's better-grounded steps. Checking sleep, medication and mood before blaming willpower fits the evidence.

### D2. "Use DBT skills- fit the facts, opposite action, loving kindness"
- **Evidence:**
  - **Moderate** for multi-week DBT-based *group programmes* in adult ADHD.
  - **Weak or none** for any single skill used alone, self-directed.
  - **Moderate** for loving-kindness meditation improving positive emotion (non-ADHD).
- **What research shows:**
  - **Name of the skill.** The DBT skill is "**check the facts**". Asking whether an emotion "fits the facts" is a step *inside* check the facts and opposite action. The chart's "fit the facts" is a garbled label.
  - **DBT-based ADHD programmes.** Hesslinger et al. 2002 adapted DBT skills into a group programme. It was an uncontrolled pilot.
  - **Positive trials.** Fleming et al. 2015 (33 college students, DBT group + coaching) and Halmøy et al. 2022 (121 adults) both beat their controls. Halmøy found gains on everyday executive function, but no difference on emotion regulation.
  - **A large null result.** The large COMPAS trial (419 adults) found its DBT-informed group therapy **did not beat** individual clinical management on ADHD symptoms (Philipsen et al. 2015).
  - **Do the skills do the work?** In BPD, DBT skills use partly explains improvement (Neacsiu et al. 2010). No study isolates check the facts or opposite action in ADHD.
  - **Loving-kindness meditation.** Medium effects on positive emotion (Zeng et al. 2015). Benefits were moderate against passive controls but inconclusive against active ones (Galante et al. 2014). It is an optional add-on in Linehan's 2015 manual, not a core DBT skill.
- **Citations:**
  - Hesslinger et al. (2002). Psychotherapy of ADHD in adults: a pilot study using a structured skills training program. *Eur Arch Psychiatry Clin Neurosci* 252. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-002-0379-0
  - Fleming et al. (2015). Pilot RCT of DBT group skills training for ADHD among college students. *J Atten Disord* 19(3). https://doi.org/10.1177/1087054714535951
  - Philipsen et al. (2015). Effects of group psychotherapy, individual counseling, methylphenidate, and placebo in adult ADHD. *JAMA Psychiatry* 72(12). https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapsychiatry.2015.2146
  - Halmøy et al. (2022). DBT-based group treatment versus treatment as usual for adults with ADHD: a multicenter RCT. *BMC Psychiatry*. https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-022-04356-6
  - Neacsiu, Rizvi & Linehan (2010). DBT skills use as a mediator and outcome of treatment for BPD. *Behav Res Ther* 48(9). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2010.05.017
  - Storebø et al. (2020). Psychological therapies for people with BPD (Cochrane). https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012955.pub2
  - Zeng et al. (2015). The effect of loving-kindness meditation on positive emotions: a meta-analytic review. *Front Psychol* 6. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.01693
  - Galante et al. (2014). Effect of kindness-based meditation on health and well-being. *J Consult Clin Psychol* 82(6). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037249
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. "Use DBT skills" packs a multi-week, therapist-taught curriculum into one line, and the label is wrong.

### D3. "Attend to the need. Go to Step 2"
- **Evidence:** It depends on the need (see D1). The logic is sound: fix sleep, hunger, medication or overwhelming emotion first. No study tests "attend to the need, then retry" as a sequence.

## Part E. Page 2: DIY DOPAMINE

### E1. "Dance break with fun song"
- **Evidence:** **Strong** that music people love triggers dopamine release. **None** that this helps them start a task.
- **What research shows:**
  - In PET imaging, dopamine was released while people listened to music that reliably gave them chills (Salimpoor et al. 2011). That shows music is rewarding. It doesn't show it transfers motivation to a dull task.
  - The short-term cognitive boost after music (the "Mozart effect") disappears once mood and arousal are accounted for (Thompson, Schellenberg & Husain 2001). So any benefit is general mood and arousal, plus light exercise (see E3).
- **Citations:**
  - Salimpoor et al. (2011). Anatomically distinct dopamine release during anticipation and experience of peak emotion to music. *Nature Neuroscience* 14. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726
  - Thompson, Schellenberg & Husain (2001). Arousal, mood, and the Mozart effect. *Psychological Science* 12(3). https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00345

### E2. "Delicious snack"
- **Evidence:** **Contradicted** that sugar or a snack boosts self-control. **Contradicted** for the sugar–hyperactivity myth.
- **What research shows:**
  - The "glucose fuels willpower" idea had limited evidential value and was probably inflated by publication bias (Vadillo et al. 2016).
  - The ego-depletion effect behind it was close to zero in a 23-lab preregistered replication (Hagger et al. 2016). Some researchers dispute the task used.
  - Sugar does not affect children's behaviour or cognition (Wolraich et al. 1995).
  - Eating when actually hungry is still sensible (see D1). It just isn't a willpower or dopamine boost.
- **Citations:**
  - Vadillo, Gold & Osman (2016). The bitter truth about sugar and willpower. *Psychological Science* 27(9). https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616654911
  - Hagger et al. (2016). A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect. *Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(4). https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616652873
  - Wolraich, Wilson & White (1995). The effect of sugar on behavior or cognition in children: a meta-analysis. *JAMA* 274(20). https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1995.03530200053037
- **Pop-science flag: STRONG.**

### E3. "Exercise: 60s jumping jacks"
- **Evidence:**
  - **Moderate** that a single exercise session gives small short-term improvements in executive function, including in children with ADHD.
  - **Weak** for a 60-second dose.
  - **None** for getting started on a task.
- **What research shows:**
  - **General population.** One exercise session has a small overall effect on cognition (79 studies; Chang et al. 2012). A single moderate aerobic session has small short-term EF effects (Ludyga et al. 2016).
  - **Children with ADHD.** A single bout gave small, short-lived EF benefits (g ≈ 0.25–0.32; Chueh et al. 2022). The landmark ADHD study used **20 minutes** of walking (Pontifex et al. 2013).
  - **Very short bouts.** The only evidence I found for 1-minute "exercise snacks" is a 25-person pilot in sedentary office workers, not ADHD, with no control group for the short-term measure (Mues et al. 2025).
  - **Long-term programmes** are a separate question. The strictest RCT meta-analysis found a small effect on ADHD symptoms (g ≈ −0.33) with low-quality evidence (Seiffer et al. 2022). Earlier reviews reported larger effects (Cerrillo-Urbina et al. 2015; Lambez et al. 2020).
- **Citations:**
  - Chang et al. (2012). The effects of acute exercise on cognitive performance: a meta-analysis. *Brain Research* 1453. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brainres.2012.02.068
  - Ludyga et al. (2016). Acute effects of moderate aerobic exercise on specific aspects of executive function. *Psychophysiology* 53(11). https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12736
  - Chueh et al. (2022). Effects of a single bout of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity on executive functions in children with ADHD. *Psychol Sport Exerc* 58. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2021.102097
  - Pontifex et al. (2013). Exercise improves behavioral, neurocognitive, and scholastic performance in children with ADHD. *J Pediatrics* 162(3). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpeds.2012.08.036
  - Mues, Flohr & Kurpiers (2025). Workplace-integrated exercise snacks and cognitive performance: a randomized pilot. *Sports* 13(6). https://doi.org/10.3390/sports13060186
  - Seiffer et al. (2022). The efficacy of physical activity for children with ADHD: a meta-analysis of RCTs. *J Atten Disord* 26(5). https://doi.org/10.1177/10870547211017982
  - Cerrillo-Urbina et al. (2015). Effects of physical exercise in children with ADHD. *Child Care Health Dev* 41(6). https://doi.org/10.1111/cch.12255
  - Lambez et al. (2020). Non-pharmacological interventions for cognitive difficulties in ADHD. *J Psychiatr Res* 120. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychires.2019.10.007
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. It's the right direction, but 60 seconds is far below the doses studied.

### E4. "Stretch"
- **Evidence:** **None** for attention or starting a task.
- **What research shows:** Micro-breaks of any kind modestly improve vigour and reduce fatigue. They don't reliably improve performance, except on easy tasks (Albulescu et al. 2022). I found no study of stretching specifically. It's harmless.
- **Citations:** Albulescu et al. (2022). "Give me a break!" A systematic review and meta-analysis on the efficacy of micro-breaks. *PLOS ONE* 17(8). https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0272460

### E5. "Novelty (position, environs)"
- **Evidence:** **Strong** that novelty activates the brain's dopamine areas. **None** that changing your position or surroundings helps you start a task.
- **What research shows:** In fMRI, the midbrain dopamine region responds specifically to *novel* stimuli (Bunzeck & Düzel 2006). These responses fade quickly with repetition, which works against relying on novelty again and again. See also C5.
- **Citations:** Bunzeck & Düzel (2006). Absolute coding of stimulus novelty in the human substantia nigra/VTA. *Neuron* 51(3). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2006.06.021
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. Real neuroscience is being stretched into an untested recommendation.

## Part F. Page 2: INTERRUPT INERTIA

### F1. "Drink water so you get up to pee", "Get into an uncomfortable position", "Create a harmless but immediate problem (toss phone across room, knock over water)"
- **Evidence:** **None** for any of the three.
- **What research shows:**
  - The general idea, using an outside cue to break a long stretch of one activity, has indirect support. Computer prompts to stand reduced long sitting bouts in office workers (Evans et al. 2012). Micro-breaks modestly improve vigour (see E4).
  - No study tests bladder-based, discomfort-based or "create a problem" interruptions.
- **Safety caveat:** Knocking over water near electronics or on floors risks electrical hazards, damaged devices and falls. Throwing a phone risks breaking it or hurting someone. A timer or alarm placed across the room uses the same logic without the risk. Drinking "lots" of water is usually harmless, but not for people on fluid restrictions, such as some heart or kidney conditions.
- **Citations (adjacent only):** Evans et al. (2012). Point-of-choice prompts to reduce sitting time at work: a randomized trial. *Am J Prev Med*. https://research.tees.ac.uk/en/publications/point-of-choice-prompts-to-reduce-sitting-time-at-work-a-randomiz/
- **Pop-science flag:** These are personal hacks presented as strategies. "Create a problem" should come with a safety note.

### F2. "Put on content blocker" / "Create interruptions: … content blockers"
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** in the general population. **None** in ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - In a field experiment with about 2,000 US adults, tools for setting limits on future screen time substantially cut use. The authors attribute about 31% of social-media use to self-control problems (Allcott, Gentzkow & Song 2022).
  - A "pause before opening" app led users to abandon about 36% of app-opening attempts. The option to dismiss the app mattered more than the delay itself (Grüning et al. 2023).
  - A review classified 367 digital self-control tools but didn't test whether they work (Lyngs et al. 2019).
- **Citations:**
  - Allcott, Gentzkow & Song (2022). Digital addiction. *American Economic Review* 112(7). https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20210867
  - Grüning, Riedel & Lorenz-Spreen (2023). Directing smartphone use through the self-nudge app one sec. *PNAS* 120(8). https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2213114120
  - Lyngs et al. (2019). Self-control in cyberspace: applying dual systems theory to a review of digital self-control tools. *CHI 2019*. https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300361

### F3. "Set a recurring alarm to check in: do you want to be doing this?" (in both boxes)
- **Evidence:** **Weak to moderate.** It rests on research into self-monitoring, i.e. tracking your own progress. ADHD-specific evidence comes only from school children.
- **What research shows:**
  - A meta-analysis of 138 RCTs found that prompting people to monitor their progress improved goal attainment (d ≈ 0.40). Effects were larger when progress was written down or reported (Harkin et al. 2016). Most of the studies were health-related.
  - Cued self-monitoring of staying on task has many single-case studies in children with ADHD.
  - Frequent interruptions raise stress and frustration (Mark, Gudith & Klocke 2008). Too many alarms can backfire.
- **Citations:**
  - Harkin et al. (2016). Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? *Psychological Bulletin* 142(2). https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025
  - Mark, Gudith & Klocke (2008). The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress. *CHI 2008*. https://www.ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf

### F4. "Ask for help to interrupt task" / "Set up accountability from others" (and in RESIST IMPULSES: "Set up accountability with others", "ask a friend for help interrupting")
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** for the general principles (public commitment, progress reporting, commitment devices). **Weak** in ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - In Harkin et al. 2016 (see F3), public reporting of progress had the largest effect (d ≈ 0.55). That estimate rests on only 3 studies.
  - People do take up commitment devices, but how well they work varies (Bryan, Karlan & Nelson 2010).
  - **Retraction.** The classic self-imposed-deadline study (Ariely & Wertenbroch 2002) was **retracted by *Psychological Science* in September 2026**. A replication was published earlier that year (Hyndman & Bisin 2026). I haven't reviewed the retraction notice's stated reasons, but **it should no longer be cited as support.**
  - For ADHD coaching I found no meta-analysis. Body doubling is covered in C7.
- **Citations:**
  - Bryan, Karlan & Nelson (2010). Commitment devices. *Annual Review of Economics* 2. https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.economics.102308.124324
  - Retraction notice: Retraction: Procrastination, deadlines, and performance: self-control by precommitment. *Psychological Science* (2026). https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261488042 (original: https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00441)
  - Hyndman & Bisin (2026). Replication of "Procrastination, deadlines, and performance". *Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261460772

### F5. "Make grumbling noises about it"
- **Evidence:** **None.**
- **What research shows:** The closest research is on *swearing*:
  - Swearing increased pain tolerance in a cold-water test (Stephens et al. 2009).
  - It slightly increased strength and power output (Stephens et al. 2018).
  - Both are physical-endurance tasks in non-ADHD adults. I found nothing on grumbling or starting tasks.
- **Citations (adjacent only):**
  - Stephens, Atkins & Kingston (2009). Swearing as a response to pain. *NeuroReport* 20(12). https://doi.org/10.1097/WNR.0b013e32832e64b1
  - Stephens, Spierer & Katehis (2018). Effect of swearing on strength and power performance. *Psychol Sport Exerc* 35. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psychsport.2017.11.014

### F6. "Pretend you're teaching someone ADHD skills & verbally explain how you overcome paralysis / resist impulses" (in both boxes)
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** for *learning and understanding* in typical students. **None** for starting tasks or for ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - Expecting to teach, and actually teaching, improves understanding (Fiorella & Mayer 2013). A meta-analysis found g ≈ 0.35 for preparing to teach and g ≈ 0.56 for teaching (Kobayashi 2019).
  - Prompting people to explain things to themselves has moderate effects on learning (Bisra et al. 2018).
  - All of this measures *learning*, not starting tasks. Talking a task through aloud plausibly helps break it into steps, but that is untested.
- **Citations:**
  - Fiorella & Mayer (2013). The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy. *Contemp Educ Psychol* 38(4). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.06.001
  - Kobayashi (2019). Learning by preparing-to-teach and teaching: a meta-analysis. *Japanese Psychological Research* 61(3). https://doi.org/10.1111/jpr.12221
  - Bisra et al. (2018). Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis. *Educ Psychol Review* 30(3). https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9434-x
- **Pop-science flag:** Mild. Claims about the "protégé effect" stretch a learning effect into a productivity effect.

## Part G. Page 2: RESIST IMPULSES

### G1. "Name the emotion, impulse, and need" (affect labelling)
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** for reducing emotional reactivity in the lab. **Weak** for reducing impulsive *behaviour*. **None** in ADHD. The "and need" part is untested.
- **What research shows:**
  - Putting feelings into words reduced amygdala responses to negative images in fMRI (Lieberman et al. 2007). These imaging links are correlational.
  - A review calls affect labelling "implicit emotion regulation", with effects similar to reappraisal (Torre & Lieberman 2018).
  - People *predict* labelling won't help, yet it reduces their distress (Lieberman et al. 2011).
  - In exposure therapy for spider fear, labelling did not reduce reported fear but did reduce a physical stress measure a week later (Kircanski et al. 2012).
- **Citations:**
  - Lieberman et al. (2007). Putting feelings into words. *Psychological Science* 18(5). https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x
  - Torre & Lieberman (2018). Putting feelings into words: affect labeling as implicit emotion regulation. *Emotion Review* 10(2). https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073917742706
  - Lieberman et al. (2011). Subjective responses to emotional stimuli during labeling, reappraisal, and distraction. *Emotion* 11(3). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023503
  - Kircanski, Lieberman & Craske (2012). Feelings into words: contributions of language to exposure therapy. *Psychological Science*. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443830
- **Pop-science flag:** Moderate. "Name it to tame it" overstates modest lab effects.

### G2. "Create physical distance from the thing"
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** for the general principle. **Contested** for phones specifically. **None** in ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - The theory says acting on the situation early beats in-the-moment willpower (Duckworth, Gendler & Gross 2016).
  - A Cochrane review found that moving food farther away cut consumption (SMD ≈ −0.60). The certainty was low and most studies were lab-based (Hollands et al. 2019).
  - People high in self-control seem to *meet fewer temptations*, rather than win more in-the-moment battles (Hofmann et al. 2012). That supports avoiding temptation over resisting it.
  - The "phone nearby drains your brain" finding (Ward et al. 2017) failed a direct replication (Ruiz Pardo & Minda 2022). A 2023 meta-analysis still reports an overall effect (Böttger et al. 2023), so the question remains disputed.
- **Citations:**
  - Duckworth, Gendler & Gross (2016). Situational strategies for self-control. *Perspect Psychol Sci* 11(1). https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691615623247
  - Hollands et al. (2019). Altering the availability or proximity of food, alcohol, and tobacco products (Cochrane). https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub2
  - Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs (2012). Everyday temptations. *JPSP*. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026545
  - Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos (2017). Brain drain. *J Assoc Consumer Research* 2(2). https://doi.org/10.1086/691462
  - Ruiz Pardo & Minda (2022). Reexamining the "brain drain" effect. *Acta Psychologica*. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103717
  - Böttger, Poschik & Zierer (2023). Does the brain drain effect really exist? A meta-analysis. *Behavioral Sciences*. https://doi.org/10.3390/bs13090751

### G3. "Create a gap. Wait just 5 minutes before acting"
- **Evidence:** **Weak** for pausing in general. **None** for "5 minutes".
- **What research shows:**
  - A brief mindfulness instruction ("urge surfing") given to 123 student smokers did not reduce how strong their urges felt, but they smoked less over the next week (Bowen & Marlatt 2009).
  - In the self-nudge app study (F2), the delay itself was a minor contributor.
  - I found no verified evidence for popular claims that "cravings pass in X minutes".
- **Citations:** Bowen & Marlatt (2009). Surfing the urge: brief mindfulness-based intervention for college student smokers. *Psychol Addict Behav* 23(4). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017127

### G4. "Look back" at the last time / "Look forward: How will I feel if I follow / don't follow X impulse?"
- **Evidence:**
  - "Look forward": **moderate**, but only for lab measures of impulsive choice in non-ADHD samples.
  - "Look back": **none**, with hints it doesn't work the same way.
- **What research shows:**
  - Imagining specific future events (episodic future thinking) reliably reduced the preference for smaller, sooner rewards across studies. The authors warn that participants guessing the study's purpose may partly explain the effect (Rung & Madden 2018).
  - People with ADHD discount future rewards more steeply (Jackson & MacKillop 2016, C4), so the technique targets a real weakness. However, I found no trial of it in ADHD.
  - One thesis found that imagining *past* events did not reduce this discounting. That undercuts "look back" as the same kind of tool (seen in a repository; not peer-reviewed).
  - People overestimate how strong and long-lasting future feelings will be, the "impact bias" (Wilson & Gilbert 2005). So "how will I feel?" is a useful question, but the answer will be a biased estimate.
- **Citations:**
  - Rung & Madden (2018). Experimental reductions of delay discounting and impulsive choice: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *J Exp Psychol: General* 147(9). https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000462
  - Wilson & Gilbert (2005). Affective forecasting: knowing what to want. *Curr Dir Psychol Sci* 14(3). https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wilson-gilbert-cdips2005.pdf
  - Thesis (not peer-reviewed): The effect of episodic future thinking on delay discounting. https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/The_effect_of_episodic_future_thinking_on_delay_discounting/17145569

### G5. "Find an out: add a natural stopping point" / "Plan an interruption"
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** in children with ADHD in the lab, via if-then plans (implementation intentions). **None** in adults or real-world settings.
- **What research shows:**
  - Children with ADHD who added an if-then plan ("if X happens, I will stop") to their goal improved their ability to stop a response. Their performance on a go/no-go task reached the level of children without ADHD, and adding stimulant medication worked best (Gawrilow & Gollwitzer 2008).
  - General if-then plan evidence: see B3.
- **Citations:** Gawrilow & Gollwitzer (2008). Implementation intentions facilitate response inhibition in children with ADHD. *Cognitive Therapy and Research*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-007-9150-1

### G6. "Make it harder to follow impulse- delete apps, hide things, etc."
- **Evidence:** **Moderate** in the general population. **None** in ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - Four weeks off Facebook raised well-being and lowered later use (Allcott et al. 2020; N = 2,844).
  - Limiting social media for 3 weeks reduced loneliness and depression in undergraduates (Hunt et al. 2018; small sample).
  - See also F2 and G2.
- **Citations:**
  - Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow (2020). The welfare effects of social media. *American Economic Review* 110(3). https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20190658
  - Hunt, Marx, Lipson & Young (2018). No more FOMO. *J Soc Clin Psychol* 37(10). https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2018.37.10.751

### G7. "Follow the dopamine and replace it with something shorter/more contained"
- **Evidence:** **None** as a tested technique. The wording is pop-science.
- **What research shows:**
  - Behaviourally, this is reward substitution: swap an open-ended reward for a time-limited one, which resembles stimulus-control methods. That is reasonable.
  - But you can't perceive or "follow" dopamine (see A3). No study tests the technique.
  - The vending-machine example works as a *delayed reward for doing the task*. In ADHD, rewards lose pull with delay (C4), so keep the gap short.
- **Pop-science flag: STRONG** for the wording.

### G8. "Do DBT worksheet on willfulness"
- **Evidence:** **None.**
- **What research shows:** Willfulness vs willingness is a clinical concept from Linehan's DBT manuals. I found no validated measure of it and no study testing it. The worksheet is only as good as the DBT package around it (D2).

## Part H. Steps ④ and ⑤

### H1. "Did you *actually* follow/try the suggestions?" (step ④)
- **Evidence:** **Not testable as stated.** Checking whether you actually used the strategies is the self-monitoring principle (F3). Reasonable, but no direct evidence.

### H2. "Set another day/time to complete the task"
- **Evidence:** **None** for rescheduling as such. **Moderate** that a *specific* if-then plan makes a rescheduled task more likely to get done.
- **What research shows:** Plans that state when, where and how have medium-to-large effects on follow-through (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, see B3). A vague "another day" is functionally procrastination. The chart's "set another day/*time*" is on the right track, but it would be stronger if it asked for a concrete if-then plan.

### H3. "Can the task be delegated? Can someone else help you?" → "Ask for help!"
- **Evidence:** **None** found for delegation or help-seeking as an ADHD task-starting strategy. Accountability evidence is in F4. It is common-sense practical advice, untested rather than disproven.

### H4. "Half-ass it. Something is better than nothing!"
- **Evidence:** **Weak.** The idea fits CBT-for-perfectionism principles, but the framing itself is untested.
- **What research shows:**
  - Perfectionistic *concerns* track procrastination (B6).
  - CBT for perfectionism reduces perfectionism, with medium-to-large effects across 15 RCTs (Galloway et al. 2022). It deliberately includes behavioural experiments in doing things "less than perfectly". The trials were few, with young samples and no active comparators.
  - Satisficing ("good enough" choosing) correlates with more happiness and less regret than maximising (Schwartz et al. 2002). That is correlational and about consumer choices.
- **Citations:**
  - Galloway et al. (2022). The efficacy of RCTs of CBT for perfectionism. *Cognitive Behaviour Therapy* 51(2). https://doi.org/10.1080/16506073.2021.1952302
  - Schwartz et al. (2002). Maximizing versus satisficing. *JPSP* 83(5). https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1178
- **Note:** The chart only reaches this after "Can you do the task partially or badly?" and before the "No, it's high-stakes" branch. That sensibly limits it to tasks where quality isn't critical.

### H5. "Practice radical acceptance. This task may not get done right now… it'll be okay."
- **Evidence:**
  - **Moderate** for acceptance-based therapy (ACT) in general.
  - **Weak** for radical acceptance specifically: two lab studies in healthy adults.
  - **Weak** for ACT in adult ADHD.
- **What research shows:**
  - ACT beat control conditions (g ≈ 0.57) and performed similarly to CBT across anxiety, depression, addiction and somatic problems (A-Tjak et al. 2015). ADHD was not included.
  - In healthy young adults, radical acceptance training improved acceptance and later reappraisal, while check the facts improved only reappraisal (Segal et al. 2023).
  - In a single session, radical acceptance brought negative mood back to baseline and check the facts did not (Segal et al. 2025).
  - In DBT, radical acceptance means accepting reality as it is, not giving up. The chart uses it correctly: it's about accepting that the task won't get done *right now*.
- **Citations:**
  - A-Tjak et al. (2015). A meta-analysis of the efficacy of ACT. *Psychother Psychosom* 84(1). https://doi.org/10.1159/000365764
  - Segal, Sher, Aderka & Weinbach (2023). Does acceptance lead to change? *Behav Res Ther* 164. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2023.104303
  - Segal, Bronshtein & Weinbach (2025). The benefits of radical acceptance of reality as a standalone strategy for emotion regulation. *Current Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s12144-025-07286-0

### H6. "Do the task. Use distress tolerance skills and harm reduction tools to minimize its impact on you."
- **Evidence:**
  - **Moderate** that distress tolerance is a real construct that can be changed.
  - **Weak** in ADHD.
  - **None** for "harm reduction" used this way.
- **What research shows:**
  - Low distress tolerance is linked to many disorders, though it is measured in at least five different ways (Leyro et al. 2010).
  - A 6-session skills programme raised distress tolerance more than control conditions in people in residential substance-use treatment (Bornovalova et al. 2012).
  - In DBT, distress tolerance skills are for getting through a *crisis* without making it worse, not specifically for getting tasks done.
  - "Harm reduction" comes from public health and addiction work (Marlatt 1996). Here it's a loose metaphor.
- **Citations:**
  - Leyro, Zvolensky & Bernstein (2010). Distress tolerance and psychopathological symptoms and disorders. *Psychological Bulletin* 136(4). https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019712
  - Bornovalova et al. (2012). Initial RCT of a distress tolerance treatment for individuals with substance use disorders. *Drug Alcohol Depend* 122. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2011.09.012
  - Marlatt (1996). Harm reduction: come as you are. *Addictive Behaviors* 21(6). https://doi.org/10.1016/0306-4603(96)00042-1

### H7. The chart's overall non-judgemental tone ("it'll be okay", self-forgiveness)
- **Evidence:** **Moderate but correlational** for the link between self-compassion and less procrastination. **Weak** for a causal effect.
- **What research shows:**
  - Across four samples, people who procrastinate less tend to be more self-compassionate, and self-compassion partly explains the link between procrastination and stress (Sirois 2014). This is correlational.
  - Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating on a first exam procrastinated less before the second. The effect was found only among heavy procrastinators (Wohl et al. 2010).
  - Self-compassion interventions improve stress, depression and anxiety (Ferrari et al. 2019). No ADHD samples.
- **Citations:**
  - Sirois (2014). Procrastination and stress: exploring the role of self-compassion. *Self and Identity* 13(2). https://doi.org/10.1080/15298868.2013.763404
  - Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010). I forgive myself, now I can study. *Pers Individ Dif* 48(7). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029
  - Ferrari et al. (2019). Self-compassion interventions and psychosocial outcomes: a meta-analysis of RCTs. *Mindfulness* 10(8). https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-019-01134-6

## Practical takeaways

1. **The best-supported parts of the chart are the unglamorous ones.**
   - Medication (when prescribed), sleep and basic needs (D1, A4).
   - Turning a vague task into a specific first step with a when/where plan (B3, H2).
   - Removing or adding friction to temptations rather than relying on willpower (F2, G2, G6).
   - Tracking or reporting progress (F3, F4).

   If you only use a few boxes, use these.
2. **Make "Set another day/time" and "1 next thing" into if-then plans**, e.g. "At 10:00 tomorrow, at my desk, I will open the file and write the heading." This is the single most consistently supported technique in this review (d ≈ 0.65 in general populations, and it has worked in lab studies of children with ADHD).
3. **Keep rewards small, frequent and close to the effort.** One big reward "for after" fits ADHD reward research less well (C4).
4. **Treat "DIY dopamine" as a mood and arousal reset, not brain chemistry.**
   - Movement is the best-supported item, but the studied doses are about 10–30 minutes, not 60 seconds.
   - Snacks don't boost willpower.
   - Drop the "dopamine" language. The activities may help; the explanation is wrong (A3, E1–E5).
5. **For background sound while working, white or pink noise has better support in ADHD than music. Lyrics and audiobooks are a poor fit for reading or writing tasks** (C1).
6. **Body doubling, Goblin Tools, "Scientist Hat", grumbling and the 5/10-minute rules are untested, not disproven.** They're low-risk to try. Keep the ones that work for you, but don't treat them as evidence-based.
7. **Skip the "harmless problem" hack, or make it safe:** use a timer across the room instead of knocking over water or throwing a phone (F1).
8. **"Use DBT skills" really means a multi-week skills course.** The DBT evidence in ADHD is for therapist-led group programmes, and the largest trial was null against individual clinical management. The skill is "check the facts", not "fit the facts" (D2).
9. **The chart's endpoints are well-judged.** "Half-ass it" (where quality allows), radical acceptance, rescheduling and a self-compassionate tone are all consistent with acceptance-based and perfectionism research, even though none has been tested in this exact form (H4–H7).
10. **If the chart keeps landing at Step ④ or ⑤ for most tasks, get a clinical assessment.** Medication and structured CBT/DBT-based programmes have far more evidence than any individual box (A4).
